Dropout & BatchNorm: two tricks inside almost every network

Dropout: while training, switch off a random part of the neurons at every step, so the network can't lean on a few of them and can't simply memorise. BatchNorm: after a layer, re-centre and re-scale its numbers so they always stay in a healthy range, which makes training faster and steadier. Both behave differently while training and while predicting. That's why model.train() and model.eval() exist.

👋 New here? The 30-second version

🧸 Think of it like this

Dropout is a football team that trains with random players sitting out each day. Everyone learns to cover for everyone, so the team doesn't fall apart without its star. BatchNorm is a teacher who grades every test "on a curve", so the scores stay comparable no matter how easy or hard the test was.

① Dropout: switch neurons off at random

At every training step, each hidden neuron is switched off with probability p. A new random set every step. The neurons that stay on are turned up by 1/(1 − p), so that the total signal stays the same size on average.

③ BatchNorm: keep every layer's numbers healthy

A deep network: 8 hidden layers of 16 tanh neurons. We send 200 points through it and draw, for every layer, which output values its neurons produce. If the starting weights are a bit too small, the numbers shrink layer after layer until they're all ≈ 0. If they're a bit too big, they pile up at −1 and +1 (flat ends, slope ≈ 0, Lesson 5). BatchNorm re-centres (average 0) and re-scales (spread 1) the sums of every layer, using the numbers of the current batch.

📏 Detailed: the math

Dropout (inverted dropout, as in PyTorch)

train: a = h · m / (1 − p), m ~ Bernoulli(1 − p)
eval: a = h

Dividing by (1 − p) during training keeps the expected value equal to h, so nothing has to change at prediction time. Dropout ≈ training a huge family of smaller networks that share weights, and averaging them at the end. In CNNs, Dropout2d drops whole feature maps instead of single numbers (Day 1 Lab Ex. 2).

BatchNorm (per neuron / per channel)

x̂ = (z − μbatch) / √(σ²batch + ε)
y = γ · x̂ + β

γ and β are learned, so the layer can still choose any average and spread it wants. During training it also keeps running averages of μ and σ² (momentum 0.1 in PyTorch). In eval() those running averages replace the batch statistics, so one image gives the same answer whatever else is in the batch. At export time Conv → BN is folded into a single conv (Day 2: w′ = w·γ/σ).

🧪 Try this (guess first, then check!)

  1. How many survive? In ①, set p = 0.5 and press +1 step a few times. How many of the 16 hidden neurons are on? How much are they turned up?
    AnswerAbout 8 of 16, a different 8 every step (the exact number varies, because each neuron flips its own coin). Survivors are turned up by 1/(1 − 0.5) = 2×. Switch to eval(): all 16 are on and nobody is turned up.
  2. The overfitting trap. In ②, keep the defaults (Moons, 40 training points, noise 40, p = 0.4) and press ▶ Train both. Compare train and test accuracy.
    AnswerA (no Dropout) hits 100% on its training points but only ≈ 79% on the test points, and its test loss climbs to ≈ 1.1: it has drawn islands around single noisy points. B (Dropout 0.4) scores lower on training (≈ 90%) but ≈ 83% on test, with a test loss of ≈ 0.44. Worse at the practice, better at the exam: that's regularization.
  3. Too much of a good thing. Set p = 0.7, then p = 0.1. Train again each time.
    Answerp = 0.1: barely any effect: B still overfits almost like A (test ≈ 78%). p = 0.7: most neurons are off every step, B becomes too weak and its border too simple (train ≈ 86%, test ≈ 82%). Common values are 0.1–0.5. Our Day-1 CNN uses 0.3 in its classifier.
  4. More data beats tricks. Set training points to 320 and train again.
    AnswerNow A and B end up close (both ≈ 86–87% test). With enough data there is little to memorise, so Dropout has less to fix. Dropout matters most when the network is big and the data is small.
  5. Shrinking and exploding. In ③, BatchNorm off. Try weight size 0.5×, then 1×, then 2×.
    Answer0.5×: each column is thinner than the one before; the last layer's spread is only ≈ 0.0005, so the signal has practically vanished. 1×: the shapes slowly shrink (spread ≈ 0.15 at the end). 2×: they swell into red blobs at −1 and +1: ≈ 60% of all outputs are stuck on a flat end, where the slope is almost 0.
  6. BatchNorm to the rescue. Now tick BatchNorm and try the same three weight sizes.
    AnswerAll three look practically the same: every layer has a wide, healthy shape (spread ≈ 0.6–0.65) and only a few percent are stuck. Because every layer is re-centred and re-scaled, the starting weight size hardly matters any more.
  7. The race. Weight size 1×, learning rate 0.1: press ▶ Race. Then try weight size 0.5×.
    AnswerWeight size 1×: without BatchNorm the loss barely moves for a long time and it needs ≈ 190 epochs to reach 95% training accuracy. With BatchNorm: ≈ 17 epochs. At 0.5× the plain network doesn't get there at all within 300 epochs (the signal has vanished, see challenge 5), while BatchNorm still needs only ≈ 30. This speed-up is the main reason BatchNorm is in almost every CNN, including MobileNetV2 (Day 2).
  8. The eval() trap. In ④, click a point near the border between the two moons. Predict 10 times in train() mode, then in eval() mode.
    Answertrain(): the 10 answers are spread out; near the border they can even land on both sides of 50%, so the predicted class changes from run to run. eval(): the same answer 10 times. Forgetting model.eval() before evaluating, exporting to ONNX or serving is one of the most common real bugs (Day 2 pitfalls list).

🐍 The same thing in PyTorch (Day 1 Lab, Exercise 2)

import torch.nn as nn

block = nn.Sequential(                        # one conv block, as in the Day-1 lab
    nn.Conv2d(1, 32, 3, padding=1),
    nn.BatchNorm2d(32),                       # after the conv, before the ReLU
    nn.ReLU(),
    nn.MaxPool2d(2),
    nn.Dropout2d(0.1),                        # drops whole feature maps
)
head = nn.Sequential(nn.Flatten(), nn.Linear(64 * 7 * 7, 128), nn.ReLU(), nn.Dropout(0.3), nn.Linear(128, 10))

model.train()                                 # Dropout on, BN uses batch stats and updates running stats
for x, y in train_loader: ...

model.eval()                                  # Dropout off, BN uses running stats
with torch.no_grad():
    preds = model(x_test).argmax(dim=1)

Two more real-world traps: frozen BatchNorm in transfer learning still updates its running statistics in train() mode (keep frozen parts in eval(), Lesson 11), and very small batches (1–4) make batch statistics noisy. Transformers use LayerNorm instead, which normalises within one sample and doesn't care about the batch.

🤔 Check yourself

  1. Why is Dropout switched off when predicting?
    AnswerWe want the full network's best, repeatable answer. Randomly missing neurons were only a training exercise, like training with weights on your ankles.
  2. With Dropout, your training accuracy is lower than without. Is that bad?
    AnswerNo. Training accuracy is measured with neurons missing and is supposed to look worse. Judge the model by validation/test accuracy (challenge 2).
  3. Your model gives different answers for the same image each time you call it. What is the most likely bug?
    AnswerIt's still in train() mode: Dropout is active (and BatchNorm uses batch statistics). Call model.eval() before predicting.
  4. What does BatchNorm "normalise", and with which numbers?
    AnswerThe sums (pre-activations) of each neuron or channel: it subtracts their average and divides by their spread. While training it uses the current batch's numbers; when predicting, the running averages saved during training.
