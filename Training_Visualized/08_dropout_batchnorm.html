Dropout & BatchNorm: when they help, and when they don't

Dropout fights overfitting: while training, it switches off random neurons, so the network can't memorise single noisy points. BatchNorm fights unstable training: it re-centres and re-scales every layer's numbers, so a deep network learns even from a bad start or with a big learning rate. Neither is magic. On this page you will see situations where each one helps, and situations where it does nothing or even hurts.

👋 New here? The 30-second version

🧸 Think of it like this

Dropout is a football team that trains with random players sitting out each day. Nobody can rely on one star, so everybody learns the game. Great for a big squad; silly for a team of four. BatchNorm is a teacher who grades every test "on a curve", so scores stay comparable however easy or hard the test was. Useless if the class has only two pupils.

① Dropout: switch neurons off at random

At every training step, each hidden neuron is switched off with probability p. A new random set every step. The neurons that stay on are turned up by 1/(1 − p), so that the total signal stays the same size on average.

Why would that help? A neuron can never be sure its neighbours are there, so the network can't build a fragile chain of neurons that exists only to fit one noisy point. Every step trains a different, thinner network, and the final network behaves like the average of all of them. Averages are smooth.

③ BatchNorm: keep every layer's numbers healthy

How it works. A deep network: 8 hidden layers of 16 tanh neurons. We send 200 points through it and draw, for every layer, which output values its neurons produce. If the starting weights are a bit too small, the numbers shrink layer after layer until they're all ≈ 0 (the signal vanishes). If they're too big, they pile up at −1 and +1 (flat ends, slope ≈ 0, Lesson 5). BatchNorm re-centres (average 0) and re-scales (spread 1) the sums of every layer, using the numbers of the current batch.

🧭 Summary: when to use them, and when not

✅ Helps when…➖ / ❌ Doesn't help, or hurts, when…Effect on the output
Dropoutthe network is big and the data is small and noisy: exactly when it would overfit. there is lots of data or little noise (nothing to fix, it only adds randomness); the network is small (it loses capacity and underfits); p is very high. Smoother predictions. Training error up, validation error down (when it helps). Needs more training steps. No cost at prediction time.
BatchNormthe network is deep, the starting weights are poorly scaled, or you want a bigger learning rate. Standard in CNNs (Day 1 Lab, MobileNetV2). the network already trains well (little or no gain); batches are tiny (1–4: the batch statistics are too noisy); you forget model.eval(). Every layer's numbers stay in a healthy range, so training works in many more settings and usually needs fewer epochs. Small extra compute (folded away at export).

📏 Detailed: the math

Dropout (inverted dropout, as in PyTorch)

train: a = h · m / (1 − p), m ~ Bernoulli(1 − p)
eval: a = h

Dividing by (1 − p) during training keeps the expected value equal to h, so nothing has to change at prediction time. Dropout ≈ training a huge family of thinner networks that share weights, and averaging them at the end (the "thinned versions" checkbox in ② draws 20 of them). In CNNs, Dropout2d drops whole feature maps (Day 1 Lab Ex. 2).

BatchNorm (per neuron / per channel)

x̂ = (z − μbatch) / √(σ²batch + ε)
y = γ · x̂ + β

γ and β are learned, so the layer can still choose any average and spread it wants. During training it also keeps running averages of μ and σ² (momentum 0.1 in PyTorch). In eval() those running averages replace the batch statistics, so one image gives the same answer whatever else is in the batch. At export time Conv → BN is folded into a single conv (Day 2: w′ = w·γ/σ).

🧪 Try this (guess first, then check!)

  1. How many survive? In ①, keep p = 0.4 and press +1 step a few times. How many of the 16 hidden neurons are on? How much are they turned up?
    AnswerOn average about 9–10 of 16 (60%), a different set every step; the exact number varies, because each neuron flips its own coin. Survivors are turned up by 1/(1 − 0.4) ≈ 1.67×. Switch to eval(): all 16 are on and nobody is turned up.
  2. Where Dropout shines. In ②, press ✅ Few noisy points, big network, then ▶ Train both. Look at both curves and at both error lines.
    AnswerA bends to reach almost every noisy dot: its training error falls to ≈ 0.042, far below the noise floor (0.16), while its validation error stays at ≈ 0.36. B's curve is calmer and closer to the green truth: training error ≈ 0.070 (it can't memorise), validation error ≈ 0.26. Worse at the practice, better at the exam: that's what "fixing overfitting" looks like.
  3. Why is B smoother? Tick "Show 20 random thinned versions of B".
    AnswerEach thin blue line is B with a different 40% of its neurons switched off. They all disagree about the noisy wiggles but agree about the big wave. The thick blue curve (eval mode, all neurons on) is roughly their average, and averaging cancels out the wiggles that only some thinned networks believe in.
  4. Not luck. Still in the ✅ situation, press 🔁 Repeat on 6 different datasets.
    AnswerDropout wins clearly on 5 of the 6 datasets and is about the same on the sixth; it never makes things worse in this situation. One lucky run proves nothing; a result that repeats is worth trusting.
  5. When it's not needed. Press ➖ Lots of data, train, then ➖ Almost no noise, train.
    AnswerLots of data: A no longer overfits (200 dots leave no room to wiggle), so there's nothing to fix: validation A ≈ 0.192, B ≈ 0.197, and "about the same" on all 6 datasets if you press 🔁. Dropout costs extra training for no gain. Almost no noise: the dots are the truth, so fitting them closely is correct. A ≈ 0.006, B ≈ 0.012: Dropout's randomness now costs accuracy.
  6. When it hurts. Press ❌ Tiny network (4 neurons) and train.
    AnswerA tiny network is too small to overfit in the first place. Switching off 40% of 4 neurons leaves only about 2 per layer each step, so B underfits: validation A ≈ 0.24, B ≈ 0.36. Dropout is for networks with capacity to spare.
  7. Shrinking and exploding. In ③, BatchNorm off. Try weight size 0.5×, then 1×, then 2×.
    Answer0.5×: each column is thinner than the one before; the last layer's spread is only ≈ 0.002, so the signal has practically vanished. 1×: the shapes slowly shrink (spread ≈ 0.20 at the end). 2×: they swell into red blobs at −1 and +1: ≈ 22% of all outputs are stuck on a flat end, where the slope is almost 0.
  8. BatchNorm to the rescue. Tick BatchNorm and try the same three weight sizes.
    AnswerAll three look the same: every layer has a wide, healthy shape (spread ≈ 0.64) and only ≈ 4% are stuck. Because every layer is re-centred and re-scaled, the starting weight size no longer matters.
  9. Where BatchNorm shines. Race ✅ Bad starting weights, then ✅ Big learning rate.
    AnswerBad starting weights: without BatchNorm the network is stuck at 50% (guessing) for all 100 epochs, because the signal vanished (challenge 7). With BatchNorm: 95% after ≈ 17 epochs. Big learning rate: without BatchNorm the steps overshoot and it never learns; with BatchNorm it reaches 95% after ≈ 7 epochs. We tried both on 4 different random starts: the plain network failed all 4 times, BatchNorm succeeded all 4 times.
  10. When it isn't needed, and when it hurts. Race ➖ Already well set up, then ❌ Tiny batches.
    AnswerWell set up: both learn fast (plain ≈ 2 epochs, BatchNorm ≈ 5). No rescue needed, no gain. Tiny batches: with only 2 points per batch, the "average and spread of the batch" are just two numbers, so they jump around wildly. The plain network reaches 95% in ≈ 7 epochs; with BatchNorm it never gets there (stuck around 40–60% on all 4 random starts we tried). Rule of thumb: BatchNorm wants batches of at least 16–32.
  11. The eval() trap. In ④, click a point near the border between the two moons. Predict 10 times in train() mode, then in eval() mode.
    Answertrain(): the 10 answers are spread out; near the border they can land on both sides of 50%, so the predicted class changes from run to run. eval(): the same answer 10 times. Forgetting model.eval() before evaluating, exporting to ONNX or serving is one of the most common real bugs (Day 2 pitfalls list).

import torch.nn as nn

block = nn.Sequential(                        # one conv block, as in the Day-1 lab
    nn.Conv2d(1, 32, 3, padding=1),
    nn.BatchNorm2d(32),                       # after the conv, before the ReLU
    nn.ReLU(),
    nn.MaxPool2d(2),
    nn.Dropout2d(0.1),                        # drops whole feature maps
)
head = nn.Sequential(nn.Flatten(), nn.Linear(64 * 7 * 7, 128), nn.ReLU(), nn.Dropout(0.3), nn.Linear(128, 10))

model.train()                                 # Dropout on, BN uses batch stats and updates running stats
for x, y in train_loader: ...               # batch_size 64: plenty for BatchNorm

model.eval()                                  # Dropout off, BN uses running stats

    preds = model(x_test).argmax(dim=1)

Two more real-world traps: frozen BatchNorm in transfer learning still updates its running statistics in train() mode (keep frozen parts in eval(), Lesson 11), and when you can only afford tiny batches (big images, detection models) people switch to GroupNorm or freeze BN. Transformers use LayerNorm, which normalises within one sample and doesn't care about the batch.

🤔 Check yourself

  1. Your model: 99% training accuracy, 80% validation accuracy, 5,000 training images, a big network. Would you add Dropout? And for 95% / 94% with a tiny network?
    AnswerFirst case: yes. The big gap is overfitting and the network has capacity to spare. Second case: no. There's no gap to close, and the tiny network would underfit even more. Try a bigger model or train longer instead (Lesson 7, "Check yourself" 3).
  2. With Dropout, your training accuracy is lower than without. Is that bad?
    AnswerNo. Training accuracy is measured with neurons missing and is supposed to look worse. Judge the model by validation/test accuracy (challenge 2).
  3. Your model gives different answers for the same image each time you call it. What is the most likely bug?
    AnswerIt's still in train() mode: Dropout is active (and BatchNorm uses batch statistics). Call model.eval() before predicting.
  4. You train with batch size 2 because the images are huge, and your BatchNorm network won't learn. Why, and what could you do?
    AnswerThe batch statistics of 2 samples are extremely noisy (challenge 10). Use a bigger batch if memory allows, gradient accumulation doesn't fix BN, so switch to GroupNorm/LayerNorm, or start from a pretrained model and keep its BN layers frozen in eval() mode.
