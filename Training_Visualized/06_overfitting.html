Underfitting, overfitting, and just right

Two students prepare for an exam. One only learns "the answer is usually C" (underfitting). The other memorises every practice question word for word, typos included (overfitting). Both fail the real exam. We want the student who learned the pattern.

🎯 The setup

The hidden truth is the smooth green curve. We only get a few noisy measurements of it: the blue training points. Our model is a polynomial, and its degree is its complexity: degree 1 is a straight line, and degree 15 can wiggle a lot. The orange validation points come from the same truth but are never used for fitting. They play the role of "the real exam".

🍝 Bias vs variance: fit 20 different training sets

Imagine collecting the training data again: 20 times, same size, new noise. We fit the same degree to each set.

  • High bias (too simple): all 20 curves agree with each other, but they are all wrong in the same way.
  • High variance (too complex): each curve chases its own noise, so the 20 curves disagree wildly.
–Bias² (avg error of the mean curve)
–Variance (how much curves disagree)

Measured on x ∈ [−0.9, 0.9]. Expected error ≈ bias² + variance + noise. A good model balances the first two.

⏱️ Early stopping: when to stop training

Now we train a degree-15 polynomial (λ = 0) with gradient descent, starting from all-zero coefficients, on the current training data. Gradient descent learns the big, smooth shape first and the noisy wiggles last. So validation error first falls, then rises again.

🧪 Try this (default: 12 points, noise 0.25, λ = 0)

  1. Walk the degree slider from 0 to 15, slowly. Watch both errors and the verdict. Which degree is best?
    AnswerTraining error keeps going down as the degree grows; it can only get better at fitting the points it sees. Validation error goes down, then shoots up. With the default data the best degree is 3 (validation ≈ 0.10, close to the noise floor 0.0625). This U-shape is the most important picture in machine learning.
  2. The perfect score trap. Set degree 11. What is the training error? Would you deploy this model?
    AnswerTraining error 0.000: the curve passes through all 12 points exactly (12 coefficients, 12 points). But look between and beyond the points: validation error is in the hundreds of thousands. A perfect training score is a warning sign, not a success.
  3. More data. Keep degree 15 and slide training points from 12 to 30 to 100.
    AnswerValidation error drops dramatically (≈ 55 → ≈ 4.6 → ≈ 0.16). With more points there is less room to wiggle between them. More data is the most reliable cure for overfitting. That's why data collection is often worth more than model tuning.
  4. Regularization. Back to 12 points, degree 15. Step λ up from 0 to 1 and watch the curve and the errors.
    Answerλ penalises large coefficients, so the curve is forced to be calmer. Validation error: ≈ 55 at λ = 0, ≈ 1.1 at 0.001, ≈ 0.34 at 0.01. At λ = 1 the curve becomes too stiff: training error rises to ≈ 0.14 (underfitting starts). In PyTorch this is the weight_decay argument of the optimizer.
  5. See bias and variance. Press 🍝 at degree 1, then degree 3, then degree 12.
    AnswerDegree 1: the 20 lines are similar (low variance) but can't follow the curve (high bias). Degree 3: close to the truth and fairly consistent, the best balance. Degree 12: a bowl of spaghetti. Variance is huge, and each fit mostly describes its own noise.
  6. Stop at the right time. Train with gradient descent (default data). Where is the best iteration? What happens after 100,000 iterations?
    AnswerValidation error is lowest after a few hundred iterations (≈ 300 steps, error ≈ 0.33 with the default data). It then rises, and by 100,000 iterations it reaches ≈ 55, the same as the exact degree-15 fit. Stopping early is a form of regularization: the model never gets time to memorise the noise. This is why training scripts track validation loss every epoch and keep the best checkpoint.

🐍 The same ideas in PyTorch

optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3, weight_decay=1e-4)   # L2 regularization

best_val = float("inf")
for epoch in range(100):
    train_one_epoch(model, train_loader, optimizer)
    val_loss = evaluate(model, val_loader)          # model.eval() + torch.no_grad() inside
    if val_loss < best_val:                         # early stopping: keep the best checkpoint
        best_val = val_loss
        torch.save(model.state_dict(), "best.pth")

Other anti-overfitting tools you will meet: data augmentation (Day 2: random crops and flips), Dropout, and smaller or pretrained models (transfer learning).

🤔 Check yourself

  1. Why can't we pick the degree using the training error?
    AnswerTraining error always prefers the most complex model (challenge 1). It can't see overfitting. Only data the model hasn't trained on can.
  2. We tried 16 degrees and picked the best one by validation error. Is that validation error an honest estimate of real-world performance?
    AnswerSlightly optimistic, because we used the validation set to make a choice. That's why we keep a third, untouched test set and look at it only once, at the very end.
  3. Your model has 95% training accuracy and 94% validation accuracy, but you need 99%. Overfitting or underfitting? What would you try?
    AnswerUnderfitting (both scores are similar and too low). Try a bigger or better model, better features, longer training, or less regularization. More data helps less here.
