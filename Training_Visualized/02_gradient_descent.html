Rolling downhill: gradient descent

You are standing on a foggy mountain and want to reach the lowest valley. You can't see far, but you can feel the slope under your feet. So you take a step downhill, feel again, step again. That is the whole algorithm.

👋 New here? The 30-second version

🧸 Think of it like this

You are blindfolded on a hill and want to reach the bottom. You can't see, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. So you take a small step downhill, feel again, step again. The computer trains a model exactly like this.

👆 Do this

  1. In Part A press Step ▶ a few times. The ball rolls down.
  2. Make the learning rate bigger (bigger steps) and press Play.
  3. Now make it very big (1.05) and press Play. Watch the ball fly away!

👀 Notice this

Small steps are slow but safe. Big steps are fast but can jump over the bottom. Steps that are too big make everything explode.

📖 Hard words, made easy:
Gradient = the slope under your feet: which way is uphillLearning rate = how big each step isOptimizer = the rule for taking stepsLocal minimum = a small dip that is not the lowest point

📐 The idea in four steps

  1. Feel the slope The slope of the loss at your current knob value is called the gradient. Positive slope means "the loss goes up if you move right".
  2. Scale it Multiply the slope by a small number called the learning rate (lr). It decides how big a step you take.
  3. Step the other way Move against the slope, because you want to go down.
  4. Repeat until the slope is (almost) flat. Flat ground means you are at the bottom of a valley.

wnew = wold − lr × gradient

Why "minus"? If the slope is +4, the loss rises to the right, so we go left (w gets smaller). If the slope is −4, the loss rises to the left, so we go right. The minus sign handles both cases automatically.

Lesson 1 had 2 knobs. A real network has millions, but the rule is the same: every knob gets its own slope and moves against it, all at the same time.

🧪 Part A challenges: predict first, then press the button

  1. Patience vs speed. Simple bowl, lr = 0.1. How many steps until w is within 0.01 of the bottom (w = 1)? Now lr = 0.01.
    AnswerAbout 28 steps with lr 0.1 and about 308 with lr 0.01. A 10× smaller learning rate needs roughly 10× more steps. Too small a learning rate wastes time (and money on the GPU bill).
  2. The magic number. Predict what happens with lr = 0.5 on the simple bowl. Then press Step once.
    AnswerIt lands exactly on the bottom in one step. The slope of (w − 1)² is 2(w − 1), so w − 0.5 × 2(w − 1) = 1. This only works because we know the exact shape of the bowl. Real losses are never this simple.
  3. Too big. Try lr = 0.9, then 1.0, then 1.05. Describe each in one word.
    Answer0.9: zig-zag (it jumps over the bottom every step but still gets closer). 1.0: stuck (it bounces between −4 and 6 forever). 1.05: explodes (each jump is bigger than the last). For this bowl the distance to the bottom is multiplied by (1 − 2·lr) every step. If that number's size is above 1, training diverges. This is what a loss = nan in your notebook usually means.
  4. The trap. Two valleys, lr = 0.05, momentum 0. Where does the ball stop? Is that the lowest point? Now set momentum 0.9 and reset. Finally try momentum 0.9 with lr 0.1.
    AnswerWithout momentum it stops at w ≈ 2.07, a local minimum (loss 1.79). The real bottom is at w ≈ −2.37 (loss −0.88). With momentum 0.9 the ball behaves like a heavy ball: it keeps its speed, rolls over the hill and finds the deeper valley. With lr 0.1 and momentum 0.9 it ends up back in the local valley. Momentum often helps, but it is not a guarantee.
  5. Many bumps. Start at 4.5 (the default). Compare momentum 0 → 0.9 → 0.95 with lr 0.05.
    AnswerMomentum 0: stuck in the first dip at 4.08. Momentum 0.9: rolls all the way to the lowest dip at −0.37. Momentum 0.95: rolls past the lowest dip and settles at −1.87. Too much momentum overshoots. PyTorch's common default is 0.9.
  6. The plateau. Flat plateau, lr = 0.3, start at −4. Watch the "Loss after each step" chart. Why is it slow at first and fast later?
    AnswerFar from the valley the ground is almost flat (slope ≈ 0.05), so the steps are tiny. Near the valley it is steep, so the steps are big. Deep networks have plateaus like this too ("vanishing gradients"). Tricks such as ReLU, good initialisation, BatchNorm and Adam exist largely to fight them.

🧠 The four optimizers in plain words

SGD: the careful walker

Step = −lr × slope. Simple and predictable. In a narrow valley it zig-zags between the walls.

Momentum: the heavy ball

Remembers its previous direction: velocity = 0.9 × old velocity + slope. It speeds up on long gentle slopes and can roll through small bumps. It may overshoot.

RMSProp: the terrain-aware walker

Divides each knob's step by how steep that knob has recently been, so steep knobs take small steps and flat knobs take bigger ones.

Adam: momentum + RMSProp

Combines both ideas. It is the most common default in deep learning (lr=1e-3) because it works reasonably well without much tuning. It is not always the fastest.

🧪 Part B challenges

  1. Narrow valley, SGD only. Untick the others. Find the largest SGD learning rate that still works. What happens at 0.5? At 0.55?
    AnswerJust below 0.5 works best (0.45 reaches the minimum in about 48 steps). At exactly 0.5 it bounces between the two walls forever; above 0.5 it explodes. The steep direction (y) limits the learning rate, and the flat direction (x) then moves painfully slowly. That is the core problem Momentum and Adam try to fix.
  2. Banana valley. Press "Use recommended". Then set SGD's learning rate to 0.01. Then try Adam at 0.05, 0.2 and 0.5.
    AnswerSGD at 0.002 reaches the minimum in about 640 steps, but at 0.01 it explodes within 3 steps. Adam reaches it with every lr from 0.05 to 0.5. Adam isn't dramatically faster here, but it is much more forgiving of your learning-rate choice. RMSProp gets into the valley but keeps jiggling and never quite reaches the minimum within 1500 steps.
  3. Four valleys. All four minima have loss 0. Race from the default start, then click four different starting points.
    AnswerDifferent starts, and even different optimizers from the same start, end in different valleys (from the default start Momentum ends near (−2.8, 3.1) while the others go to (−3.8, −3.3)). All are equally good here. Real networks also have many different, equally good solutions. That's why two training runs with different random seeds give different weights but similar accuracy.
  4. The saddle. Select Saddle point and race. Where do they stop? Is the loss the lowest possible? Now set gradient noise to 0.1 and race again.
    AnswerThey all stop at (0, 0) with loss 0, but the real minima are at y = ±1.41 with loss −1. At (0, 0) the slope is exactly zero in every direction (a saddle point: downhill along y, uphill along x), so no optimizer gets a signal to move. With a little noise they fall off the saddle and find a real minimum. This is one reason the "noise" of mini-batches is actually useful.

🐍 In PyTorch

import torch

optimizer = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.1)                 # careful walker
optimizer = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.1, momentum=0.9)   # heavy ball
optimizer = torch.optim.RMSprop(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)              # the usual default

# inside the training loop, this one line does "w = w - lr * gradient" for EVERY parameter:
optimizer.step()

The Momentum formula here is exactly PyTorch's: v = momentum·v + g, then w = w − lr·v. Adam uses PyTorch's defaults β₁ = 0.9 and β₂ = 0.999; RMSProp uses α = 0.99.

🤔 Check yourself

  1. Your training loss suddenly becomes nan after 200 steps. What is the first thing you try?
    AnswerLower the learning rate (for example ×0.1). Divergence, like lr 1.05 on the bowl, is the most common cause. Other causes: bad input data (a nan in the dataset), or log(0) in a hand-written loss.
  2. The gradient at your current point is exactly 0. Are you guaranteed to be at the best solution?
    AnswerNo. You could be at a local minimum (two valleys), a saddle point, or on a flat plateau. A zero gradient only means "flat here".
  3. Why don't we just compute the exact minimum with a formula, like the 🤖 button in Lesson 1 could?
    AnswerFor a straight line there is a formula. For a neural network with millions of parameters and nonlinear activations there is no formula, so we must walk downhill step by step.
