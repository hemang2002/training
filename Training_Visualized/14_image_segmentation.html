Image segmentation: a label for every pixel

A classifier says what is in a picture. A segmentation model says what every single pixel is: "this pixel is person, this one is road". The answer is a second picture, the mask, with the same size as the photo. This page shows how such a model is built (an encoder–decoder, like U-Net) and how we check if its mask is any good (IoU).

👋 New here? The 30-second version

🧸 Think of it like this

A colouring book. Classification is saying "this page shows a person". Segmentation is colouring in the person, carefully, right up to the lines. You need to know what it is and where its edges are.

👀 Notice this

The model first shrinks the picture (to understand it) and then grows it back (to draw the mask). Without the "skip" bridges, the mask becomes a blob. And a mask can be 95% "accurate" while missing every person.

③ How the model works: shrink, then grow back (U-Net)

Our task: person vs background (2 classes), like the notebook. Press Next ▶ to follow a picture through the model.

Walk through

Why the U shape?

🔽 Going down, the maps get smaller but deeper: each number sees a bigger part of the picture → the model understands WHAT.
🔼 Going up, maps get bigger again → the model can say WHERE.
🌉 Skip bridges bring back the sharp details that were lost on the way down.

Encoder–decoder with skip connections (U-Net). Size = pixels per side · ch = number of feature maps
👀 These two outputs are drawn to show the expected behaviour (not a real trained model). Without skips the decoder only has the tiny 16 × 16 map: legs melt together, the two people blur, edges go round. With skips the edges come back.

📏 How is it trained?

Just like a classifier, but once per pixel. For every pixel, the model gives a probability for each class. The loss is the cross-entropy of each pixel (Lesson 5), averaged over all pixels. A 128 × 128 picture = 16,384 small classification problems at once.

🎁 The encoder is a normal classification CNN. So we can use a pretrained backbone (Lesson 11), such as MobileNetV3 or ResNet trained on ImageNet, and only teach the decoder.

🎓 Detailed: the Dice loss

If people cover only 5% of the pixels, plain cross-entropy is mostly about background. The Dice loss looks at the overlap of the person mask directly:

Dice = 2·|P ∩ T| / (|P| + |T|) Dice loss = 1 − Dice P = predicted mask (soft), T = true mask

It is often used together: loss = CE + Dice loss. Real U-Nets use two 3×3 convs + BatchNorm + ReLU per block, max-pooling (or stride 2) to go down, transposed convolutions or bilinear upsampling to go up, and torch.cat for the skips. DeepLabV3 and LR-ASPP are lighter "encoder + small decoder" designs of the same idea.

⑤ The case study: finding people in street photos (Notebook 06)

ℹ️ This section describes what we expect to happen in the notebook. These are typical tendencies, not measured numbers. Run the notebook to see your own.
  1. 📦 The data: Penn-Fudan pedestriansAn open-source dataset: 170 street photos, and for each one a mask with the people drawn in. That is very few pictures for deep learning.
  2. 🔄 Augment picture and mask togetherIf you flip the photo, you must flip the mask the same way (try it in the flip demo). Same for crops and rotations. Colour changes only touch the photo.
  3. 🎁 Pretrained model, out of the boxLoad a torchvision model (LR-ASPP MobileNetV3 or DeepLabV3) that was trained on COCO with the 21 VOC classes. It already knows "person": expect good masks with no training at all.
  4. 🧱 Our own small U-Net, from scratchRandom weights, 170 pictures. Expect it to find roughly where people are, but with blobby edges and a clearly lower IoU. It has too few examples to learn good features (Lesson 11).
  5. 🔧 Fine-tune the pretrained modelReplace its last layer with a 2-class head (person vs background) and train gently. Expect the best result: pretrained features + our exact task.
  6. 📏 Measure with IoUOn test photos the model never saw. Not pixel accuracy (see ④).

🚗 Self-driving carsWhich pixels are road, lane, car, person? Needed to plan where to drive.
🩻 Medical scansOutline a tumour or an organ in an MRI / CT scan, to measure its size.
🤳 Photo appsBackground blur and "remove background": segment the person, keep only those pixels.
🛰️ Satellite mapsColour in buildings, roads, water and fields from space photos.

🧪 Try this (guess first, then check!)

  1. The lazy model. In ④, press 🚫 All background. Guess the pixel accuracy and the IoU before you look.
    AnswerPixel accuracy ≈ 95%, IoU = 0. The people are only ≈ 5% of the pixels, so "background everywhere" is right almost everywhere, and useless. That's why segmentation is measured with IoU (or Dice) per class, not with pixel accuracy. Same lesson as imbalanced data in Lesson 9.
  2. A small mistake. In ④, keep the person shape and shift it only 6 pixels to the right. How much does IoU drop? Now try the same with the size slider.
    AnswerIoU drops a lot, to about 0.5, even though the mask "looks almost right". People are thin (arms, legs), so a small shift moves much of the mask off the person. Thin objects are hard: every pixel of edge error counts. Notice Dice is always higher than IoU for the same mask.
  3. Cut the bridges. In ③, go to step 6 (skip connections) and tick ✂️ Remove skip connections. What happens to the legs and the two people?
    AnswerThe decoder only has the 16 × 16 bottleneck. One cell covers 8 × 8 pixels, so the gap between the legs (and the edges) is simply not stored any more. Upsampling can't bring back detail that is gone: you get round blobs. Skip connections copy the sharp, full-size encoder maps across, so the decoder knows where the edges are.

🐍 The same thing in PyTorch (Notebook 06)

import torch, torch.nn as nn
from torchvision.models.segmentation import lraspp_mobilenet_v3_large, LRASPP_MobileNet_V3_Large_Weights

weights = LRASPP_MobileNet_V3_Large_Weights.DEFAULT                 # pretrained on COCO, 21 VOC classes
model = lraspp_mobilenet_v3_large(weights=weights)
model.eval()                              # inference mode
out = model(x)['out']                  # [B, 21, H, W]: a score per class, per pixel
mask = out.argmax(dim=1)               # [B, H, W]: class id per pixel (15 = person)

# fine-tune: new 2-class head (0 = background, 1 = person)
model.classifier.low_classifier  = nn.Conv2d(40,  2, kernel_size=1)
model.classifier.high_classifier = nn.Conv2d(128, 2, kernel_size=1)
loss_fn = nn.CrossEntropyLoss()           # works per pixel: logits [B,2,H,W], target [B,H,W] (long)
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4)   # small learning rate (Lesson 11)

def iou(pred, true, cls=1):
    p, t = pred == cls, true == cls
    return (p & t).sum() / (p | t).sum().clamp(min=1)

A U-Net decoder step: x = up(x); x = torch.cat([x, skip], dim=1); x = conv_block(x). The cat is the skip connection. Augmentation: use torchvision.transforms.v2 with the image and a tv_tensors.Mask together, so flips and crops hit both.

🤔 Check yourself

  1. A segmentation model works on 256 × 256 photos with 3 classes (background, road, car). What is the shape of its raw output, and of the final mask?
    AnswerRaw output: 3 × 256 × 256 scores (one score per class per pixel). After argmax per pixel: a 256 × 256 mask of class numbers 0, 1 or 2.
  2. You flip your training photos left–right for augmentation, but forget to flip the masks. What happens?
    AnswerThe labels are wrong for every flipped photo: the mask says "person" where there is road. The model learns from contradicting answers and gets worse, not better. Geometric changes must hit photo and mask together.
  3. Why does a segmentation model first shrink the picture, if it has to output a full-size mask anyway?
    AnswerShrinking lets each number "see" a big part of the picture, so the model can understand what things are (a person, not just a red patch). It is also much cheaper. The decoder and skip connections then recover the exact positions.
  4. You want to count how many people are in a crowd photo. Semantic or instance segmentation?
    AnswerInstance segmentation: it gives each person their own mask. Semantic segmentation colours all people the same, so touching people merge into one blob and you can't count them.
